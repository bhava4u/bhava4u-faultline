Megaspeed and the Nvidia chip controversy: how export controls leak through Southeast Asia

Faultline Analysis  |  13 November 2026  |  6 min read

A Singapore cloud company at the centre of the Nvidia scandal shows how restricted AI chips can reach end users that US rules are meant to exclude, and why intermediaries in third countries are now the weak point of export controls.

Who Megaspeed is

Megaspeed International is a Singapore-based cloud computing provider that rents out AI computing power. It was incorporated in 2015 as 7Road International, a Chinese gaming firm with ties to state-backed investors, and renamed Megaspeed in March 2023. Another Singapore firm, Swiftdata, acquired it in November 2023, and Singaporean businessman Tan Yong Pong became its sole director. In 2024 he set up Speedmatrix, a Malaysian subsidiary reportedly used to receive Nvidia chip shipments, and Megaspeed operates data centres in Johor’s Sedenak Tech Park. It describes itself as an Nvidia Cloud Partner and advertises GB200 NVL72 and HGX H100 systems.

What is alleged

Media investigations suggest Megaspeed bought billions of dollars of Nvidia AI chips and that some capacity ended up serving Chinese companies. The New York Times reported that Speedmatrix received more than 200 shipments of restricted Nvidia technology between June 2024 and June 2025. The Information reported that at least $7.5 billion of chips moved through Megaspeed and Speedmatrix, and that some powered data centres in China run by a firm linked to a relative of a former Megaspeed executive.

Singapore police are investigating Megaspeed for suspected breaches of domestic law, and US officials have examined potential export-control violations. None of these allegations has been proven. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, and Nvidia has said it found no evidence of illicit chip transfers. On 12 November Megaspeed said it had hired a US law firm to audit its GPUs and verify its export-control compliance.

The case has drawn wider attention because of reports about a personal relationship between Nvidia’s chief executive and a former Megaspeed executive, and US claims that she worked for Chinese intelligence. China denies the claims, Singapore says it is not investigating her for espionage, and Nvidia’s board has opened an independent investigation, saying it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised.

Why Singapore and Malaysia

US rules bar exports of top-end Nvidia chips to China, but it is currently legal for Chinese firms to rent computing power housed outside China. That creates two routes to restricted silicon: renting capacity in third-country data centres, which is lawful today, and buying chips through front companies and shipping them onward, which may not be. Malaysia has drawn about $94 billion of data-centre investment since 2021, and Singapore is a regional headquarters hub, which makes both attractive for legitimate business and for anyone trying to get around the rules.

Enforcement is tightening

Oracle reportedly cancelled a $7 billion deal to give Tencent access to AI chips in Southeast Asian data centres after US officials raised concerns. Nvidia is reportedly requiring cloud clients to sell most of their services to non-Chinese customers. The US House has passed the Remote Access Security Act to close the rental loophole, and in October Malaysian customs seized $12.1 million of AI servers falsely declared at Kuala Lumpur airport.

Faultline view: Whatever investigators conclude about Megaspeed, the case shows that export controls depend on intermediaries vetting customers they rarely verify. For chipmakers, cloud providers, investors and lenders, Southeast Asian AI infrastructure now carries regulatory and reputational risk that ordinary due diligence can miss.

Red flags for due diligence teams

Name changes and origins. A company renamed and repurposed shortly before a rapid scale-up deserves questions about who controls it.

Capacity that does not match imports. Reports suggest hardware volumes outpaced declared data-centre capacity, a classic sign that goods are moving on.

Related parties. Links between suppliers, executives and end users, including family ties, need mapping across jurisdictions.

New affiliates in unexpected places. Affiliates registered in 2024 in the US and Australia illustrate how quickly a corporate network can spread.

What to watch

The outcome of Singapore’s investigation and any US enforcement action; the results of Megaspeed’s compliance audit and Nvidia’s board inquiry; and whether the US Senate passes the Remote Access Security Act.
