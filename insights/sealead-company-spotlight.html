SeaLead: how a fast-growing container line ran into US sanctions

Faultline Analysis  |  13 November 2026  |  5 min read

In two years SeaLead went from one of the world’s fastest-growing container lines to voluntary liquidation. Its collapse is a case study in how sanctions risk reaches companies through their vessels, partners and ownership networks, and why one-off checks are not enough.

Who SeaLead is

Sea Lead Shipping Pte. Ltd. is a privately owned container line founded in 2017 and headquartered in Singapore, with a registered address on Shenton Way. It also operated through companies in Dubai, the Marshall Islands and India. Alphaliner ranked it the fastest-growing container line in 2024 and the 13th largest in 2025.

Timeline

July 2025. The US Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) sanctioned 16 container ships chartered by SeaLead, linking them to the shipping network of Mohammad Hossein Shamkhani, a former IRGC Navy commander and son of senior Iranian official Ali Shamkhani. SeaLead terminated the charters and denied links to Iran.

14 July 2026. OFAC designated SeaLead itself, along with subsidiaries in the UAE, the Marshall Islands and India, as part of action against more than 50 people, companies and ships. OFAC alleged SeaLead managed container operations carrying both legitimate and illicit cargo, including shipments linked to the Houthis. It also blocked three ships it operated: Shenton Way and Tanjong Pagar 1 (Panama-flagged) and Paya Lebar (Antigua and Barbuda). Treasury says it has now sanctioned more than 200 people, entities and vessels tied to the Shamkhani network.

After designation. SeaLead entered voluntary liquidation, saying it remained solvent. By then its fleet had shrunk to a handful of ships, and it had fallen to 80th in Alphaliner’s ranking.

What its online footprint shows

We ran a passive open-source scan of SeaLead’s website domain, sea-lead.com, using SpiderFoot. It found:

A domain registered in June 2017, matching the company’s founding, and paid up until 2029 despite the liquidation.

Hidden ownership. The registration uses a privacy service, and the site sits behind Cloudflare. Both are common and not suspicious in themselves, but they mean ownership must be established through company registries, not web records.

A UAE footprint in its email setup. SeaLead’s email security record authorises a UAE-region email-signature service, consistent with the Dubai operations OFAC also designated.

Most of the scan’s other results were noise from shared hosting, which is a reminder that automated OSINT produces leads to verify, not conclusions.

Faultline view: SeaLead shows how sanctions exposure can build in stages: first through chartered vessels, then through the operator itself. For anyone shipping with, chartering to or financing a carrier like this, the warning signs came a year before the company was designated.

Lessons for due diligence

Screen the fleet, not just the company. SeaLead’s first exposure came through ships it chartered, not its own listing.

Monitor continuously. A clean check in early 2025 would have been out of date by July.

Follow the subsidiaries. The designation covered entities in four jurisdictions; each needs its own checks.

Treat rapid growth as a question, not only a strength. Unusually fast expansion in a sanctions-sensitive trade deserves a closer look at who is behind it.

What to verify next

Company records in Singapore’s ACRA registry, including directors and liquidation status; the exact names of the designated subsidiaries in OFAC’s sanctions list; and vessel histories for the three blocked ships in free databases such as Equasis.
